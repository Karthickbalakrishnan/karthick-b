02
Research Planning · Double Diamond · What Comes First

The Research Roadmap — Sequenced for Evidence

In complex, high-stakes financial UX, the order of research activities is as important as the activities themselves. Talking to users before understanding the business context produces misaligned insights. Testing before diagnosing produces solutions to the wrong problem.

Wks 1–2
Stakeholder Alignment Interviews — Internal First, Always

Business goals, regulatory constraints (RESPA, ECOA, Reg B), operational pain points. Loan officers, branch managers, call centre leads, compliance, digital product team. Output: Research Brief + Stakeholder Mental Model Map.

Wks 3–4
Analytics Funnel Audit + Session Replay Analysis (n=340)

Establish quantified baseline before qualitative exploration. 90-day Google Analytics export. 340 Hotjar session recordings coded for rage-clicks, hover hesitations, back-button exits. Output: Annotated Friction Heatmap + Baseline Funnel.

Wks 5–6
Exit Intent Survey (n=320) + Call Centre Transcript Analysis (n=200)

Quantitative validation of session replay hypotheses. Exit survey coded thematically. Call transcripts coded for reason-for-call. Output: Coded Exit Themes + Call Reason Matrix.

Wks 7–8
End-User Recruitment + Screener Design — Four User Types

20 participants total: 5 per type. First-time buyers (Guest), existing members (Refinancers), Co-applicant pairs, Military/VA. Recruited via PenFed database + Respondent.io. Compensation: $75 gift card per 90-min session.

Wks 9–10
Moderated Usability Testing — Think-Aloud on Existing App (n=20)

Observe real behaviour before proposing solutions. 90-min sessions via Lookback.io. NASA-TLX post-session. 5E framework observation coding. Output: Verbatim Transcripts + NASA-TLX Scores + Affinity Map.

Wks 11–12
Loan Officer Deep-Dive Interviews (n=8) + Workflow Shadowing

Secondary user research — internal loan officers. This was the step missing from the original brief. 60-min semi-structured interviews + 2-hour workflow shadowing in CRM. Critical for designing the Readiness Score and LO Engine. Output: LO Workflow Map + Mental Model Document.

Wks 13–14
Competitive Analysis + Card Sorting (IA Research, n=24)

Competitive analysis of Rocket Mortgage, Better.com, Chase, SoFi on 12 UX dimensions. Unmoderated card sort via Optimal Workshop: 48 cards (form fields) grouped by users. Output: Competitive Matrix + Card Sort Dendrogram + Field Groupings.

Wks 15–16
Synthesis — Affinity Mapping + Persona Development + IA Design

FigJam affinity mapping with researcher team + 2 PenFed stakeholders. Thematic clusters → Insight statements → Design principles → Personas → IA. JTBD framework applied. Output: 4 Personas, 4 Empathy Maps, IA Sitemap.

Wks 17–20
Design — Lo-fi → Mid-fi → High-fi Prototype

Iterative design. Mid-fi chatbot wizard tested and FAILED (see Section 16). Revised to grouped-panel approach based on user data. High-fi prototype in Figma + React SPA parallel dev. Output: Design System + React Component Library.

Wks 21–26
Validation — Moderated UT (Redesign, n=16) + A/B Test (n=2,400) + 90-Day Post-Launch

NASA-TLX re-administered. 6-week A/B test with live traffic. Secondary A/B isolating the LO Engine. Output: Outcome Metrics + ACM Paper + Final Case Study.

Stakeholder Research · Internal Perspective Before External

The Business Reality Behind the Form

We interviewed 14 internal stakeholders across five functions before recruiting a single external user. Stakeholder alignment is the condition under which user research produces actionable insights rather than orphaned recommendations.

Participant Profile
4
Loan Officers

Senior LOs who receive and process submitted applications. 2 in-branch, 2 remote. 45–90 min sessions + 2-hour workflow shadowing. Critical secondary users.

2
Branch Managers

Responsible for conversion targets. Focused on completed application quality and applicant relationship management.

3
Call Centre Leads

First point of contact for post-submit status queries. Highest visibility of the post-submit UX failure — they lived the consequences of the generic confirmation message daily.

2
Compliance Officers

RESPA, ECOA, Regulation B, HMDA. Non-negotiables for disclosure, adverse action notice, fair lending. Pre-loan letter must be "Conditional Pre-Approval" per ECOA.

3
Digital Product Team

Platform owners. React SPA feasibility, Plaid/SSN API constraints, PenFed database connectivity, capacity, and timeline.

Key Stakeholder Insights
Loan Officers — The Defining Finding

"I get 30–40 applications per week. At least half are missing at least one document. I spend the first 20 minutes of every application review figuring out what is missing and calling to ask. There is no structure to what I receive." — LO Interview #2. This single quote directly shaped the Application Readiness Score.

Call Centre Leads

"On Mondays we have a weekend queue. 70–75% ask the same three questions: Did you get my application? Who will be calling me? What documents do you still need? We have to look up the file manually each time. Every call takes 6–10 minutes." — Call Centre Lead #1

Compliance Officers — Non-Negotiables

Three mandatory disclosures before submission: HMDA data collection, Patriot Act notice, E-sign consent. The pre-loan success letter must be labelled "Conditional Pre-Approval" — not "Approval" — per ECOA prohibition on misleading credit communications.

Stakeholder Interview Guide
Current State (15 min)

Walk me through what happens after an application is submitted. What is the first thing you do when you open a new application? What percentage arrive complete? What is the most common missing item?

Pain Points (20 min)

Where do you spend the most time on a single application? What information are applicants most confused about? If you could change one thing about how applications arrive, what would it be?

Mental Models (15 min)

How do you judge whether an application is "ready to process"? What is your personal checklist? How do you currently communicate with applicants about missing items?

Success Criteria (10 min)

What would a perfectly submitted application look like? How would a tool that shows you an application readiness score change your workflow before you open the file?

End-User Research · Four User Types · Interview Framework

Understanding Who Actually Uses This Form

A mortgage application serves fundamentally different people with different contexts, knowledge levels, document situations, and emotional states. Designing for a single "average user" produces a form that serves no user well.

🏠

Type A — First-Time Buyer (Guest)

Screener: Planning first home purchase within 12 months. No prior mortgage experience. No existing PenFed account. Mix of employed + self-employed. Age 27–42. N=5.

Rationale: Highest anxiety, lowest financial literacy baseline, greatest need for orientation and trust-building. Primary design challenge.

🔄

Type B — Existing Member (Refinancer)

Screener: Current PenFed member with active loan. Planning to refinance. 2+ years membership. N=5.

Rationale: The "recognition" problem. Established relationship users expect institutional recognition. Abandonment driven by disrespect, not anxiety.

👥

Type C — Co-Applicant Pairs

Screener: Married/partnered couples planning a joint application. One primary decision-maker, one secondary contributor. N=3 pairs (6 individuals), tested as pairs.

Rationale: The coordination problem. Applications requiring two people's data with no infrastructure for distributed completion.

🎖

Type D — Military / VA Applicant

Screener: Active duty, veteran, or surviving spouse. VA loan eligible. PenFed military member. May apply from overseas time zone.

Rationale: PenFed's heritage user. VA documentation requirements, military income types (BAH, BAS), and potential time-zone constraints on the 9–5 contact window.

Opening Context (10 min)

Tell me about your home buying journey. What made you decide to apply for a mortgage now? Have you applied anywhere else? Walk me through what happened when you first thought about applying online.

Application Walk-Through — Think Aloud (25 min)

Please open the PenFed mortgage application as you would at home. Think aloud as you go. Observer codes: hesitations, re-reads, back-button exits, rage-clicks, verbalised confusion. Stop whenever you would stop in real life.

Mental Model Probing (15 min)

Before today, what did you think applying for a mortgage online would involve? What information did you expect to need? How long did you expect it to take?

Document Mental Model (10 min)

If I asked you to apply for a mortgage right now, what documents would you reach for first? Probes: Why that one? What if you did not have it? Would you start without it? Is document readiness on/off or a spectrum?

Post-Submit Mental Model (10 min)

After clicking Submit, what do you expect to happen next? How would you feel receiving "A loan officer will contact you during business hours"? What information would give you the most confidence after submitting?

Type-Specific Probes

Type B (Member): "When the system asks for your address — the one PenFed already has — how does that make you feel?"
Type C (Co-App): "If you started tonight and your partner was not home, would you? Why/why not?"
Type D (Military): "When it says call between 9–5 — when do you have phone access?"

Research Methods · Full Inventory · Rationale + Sequencing

Why Each Method, Why in This Order

Every research method is a tool with a specific job. The discipline is knowing which tool to reach for and when — and being able to justify that choice to stakeholders who want to skip directly to solutions.

Method 01 · Qualitative Primary

Stakeholder Interviews

  • Why: Align business intent before user research. Prevents solutions that contradict regulatory or operational constraints.[cite: 1]
  • How: Semi-structured 60-min sessions. 5 functional groups. Four question areas: current state, pain points, mental models, success criteria.[cite: 1]
  • When: Weeks 1–2. Always before user contact.[cite: 1]
  • Output: Stakeholder mental model map, constraint list, business metrics.[cite: 1]
Method 02 · Quantitative Secondary

Analytics Funnel Audit

  • Why: Establish the quantified baseline before qualitative work. Prevents confirmation bias in user interviews.[cite: 1]
  • How: 90-day Google Analytics export. Stage-by-stage abandonment rates computed.[cite: 1]
  • When: Weeks 3–4. Before session replay or user interviews.[cite: 1]
  • Output: Quantified baseline funnel. Primary and secondary exit points identified.[cite: 1]
Method 03 · Behavioural Observational

Session Replay Analysis

  • Why: Reveals what users DO, not what they say they do. Rage-clicks and hover hesitations are physiological residues of cognitive friction not accessible via self-report.[cite: 1]
  • How: 340 sessions via Hotjar. Coded for rage-clicks, hover pauses, back-button exits, SSN abandonment.[cite: 1]
  • When: Weeks 3–4. Alongside analytics to triangulate patterns.[cite: 1]
  • Output: Friction heatmap, rage-click cluster locations.[cite: 1]
Method 04 · Quantitative Attitudinal

Exit Intent Survey

  • Why: Captures stated reason for abandonment at the moment of exit. n=320 provides statistical validity for theme frequency claims.[cite: 1]
  • How: Triggered on cursor-to-tab movement. 4 closed questions + 1 open text. Thematic analysis coding.[cite: 1]
  • When: Weeks 5–6. After analytics and replay to validate behavioural hypotheses.[cite: 1]
  • Output: Abandonment theme frequency table, verbatim quote bank.[cite: 1]
Method 05 · Qualitative Primary

Moderated Usability Testing — Think-Aloud

  • Why: Observes real-time cognitive processing. The think-aloud protocol externalises internal reasoning, revealing not just where users fail but why at that exact moment.[cite: 1]
  • How: 20 participants × 90 min via Lookback.io. Task: complete mortgage application. 5E framework observation. NASA-TLX post-session.[cite: 1]
  • When: Weeks 9–10 (existing app). Weeks 21–22 (redesign prototype).[cite: 1]
  • Output: Verbatim transcripts, NASA-TLX scores, failure taxonomy.[cite: 1]
Method 06 · Psychophysical Validated Scale

NASA Task Load Index

  • Why: Standardised, validated, multidimensional cognitive load measurement. Six dimensions (Mental, Physical, Temporal, Performance, Effort, Frustration) reveal which aspects of load drive the composite score.[cite: 1]
  • How: Hart and Staveland (1988) paper-based version. Weighted rating procedure. Administered post every usability session.[cite: 1]
  • When: After every UT session — both before and after redesign.[cite: 1]
  • Output: Before: 72.4 composite. After: 47.8. Per-dimension comparison enables targeted design justification.[cite: 1]
Method 07 · Competitive Benchmark

Competitive Analysis

  • Why: Users compare PenFed against Rocket Mortgage, Better.com, and Chase. Understanding the competitive baseline sets the minimum bar the redesign must clear to avoid negative comparison effects.[cite: 1]
  • How: 12-dimension evaluation rubric across 5 competitors. Dimensions: welcome, step visibility, doc handling, co-app flow, save mechanism, SSN trust, post-submit, mobile quality.[cite: 1]
  • When: Weeks 13–14. After user research to evaluate competitors through the lens of known needs.[cite: 1]
  • Output: PenFed ranked last on 7 of 12 dimensions.[cite: 1]
Method 08 · IA Research

Card Sorting (n=24)

  • Why: The legacy form's question order was determined by database schema. Card sorting reveals the user's natural IA — the groupings they find intuitive, not the groupings a developer found convenient.[cite: 1]
  • How: Unmoderated open card sort via Optimal Workshop. n=24. 48 cards (form fields). Dendrogram analysis + similarity matrix.[cite: 1]
  • When: Weeks 13–14. Results directly informed the 6-step IA redesign and the grouped-panel approach.[cite: 1]
  • Output: Field grouping dendrogram, natural label vocabulary, IA restructuring.[cite: 1]
Method 09 · Expert Systematic

Heuristic Evaluation

  • Why: Two trained evaluators applying Nielsen's 10 heuristics to all 33 steps produces a systematic expert audit complementing user research. Identifies violations that may not surface in limited-sample usability sessions.[cite: 1]
  • How: Two independent evaluators. Severity 0–4. 7 findings at severity 3–4 (critical/major). Reconciled and prioritised.[cite: 1]
  • When: Week 15. After user research to frame evaluator attention on known problem areas.[cite: 1]
  • Output: Heuristic violation log with severity ratings and design recommendations.[cite: 1]

Psychological Frameworks · Applied Behavioural Science

The Theory Behind Every Design Decision

Every structural decision in this redesign is the application of a specific, research-backed psychological principle. This is not post-hoc rationalisation — each framework was selected before the corresponding design decision was made.

🧠

Cognitive Load Theory — Sweller, 1988

Applied to: 6-step IA, progressive doc disclosure, inline validation, income grouped panels.

Principle: Eliminate extraneous load (UI-imposed friction). Preserve intrinsic load (inherent task complexity). Protect germane load (productive cognitive effort). Each step has a single cognitive focus.

🔄

Dual-Process Theory — Kahneman, 2011

Applied to: Welcome screen, SSN trust layer, pre-qualification signal, post-submit commitment.

Principle: System 1 (fast, emotional) vetoes the application before System 2 (deliberate) can evaluate it. Every trust signal, progress indicator, and specific commitment prevents the System 1 veto.

🎯

Goal Gradient Effect — Kivetz et al., 2006

Applied to: 6-step progress bar with step number, name, time remaining.

Principle: Completion motivation increases as individuals perceive proximity to a goal. The progress bar creates the psychological experience of "almost there" from Step 3 onward, driving completion acceleration.

📉

Loss Aversion — Kahneman and Tversky, 1979

Applied to: Auto-save, pre-loan success letter, Application Readiness Score.

Principle: Losses felt approximately 2x as strongly as equivalent gains. Auto-save eliminates the threat of total effort loss. The pre-loan letter creates an endowment the user does not want to lose.

🤝

Commitment and Consistency — Cialdini, 1984

Applied to: Pre-qualification signal at Step 1, slot booking, calendar invite.

Principle: Once people commit to an identity ("I am someone who qualifies for a mortgage"), they act consistently with that identity. Subsequent completion becomes identity-consistent behaviour.

↕️

Reciprocity — Cialdini, 1984

Applied to: SSN trust messaging, named LO slot, pre-loan success letter.

Principle: When an institution gives something specific and personal, the recipient feels obliged to reciprocate. SSN trust + named LO slot + pre-loan letter = PenFed's reciprocal offer for the user's complete application.

💼

Jobs-to-be-Done — Christensen, 2016

Applied to: Persona framing, step naming, engagement copy.

Principle: Users hire the application to do a job: move them toward home ownership. "Your dream home is closer than you think" speaks to the job (home ownership), not the tool (mortgage application).

🔒

Self-Efficacy Theory — Bandura, 1977

Applied to: Upload failure states, progress bar, inline validation, pre-qualification signal.

Principle: Perceived capability to perform a task determines whether a person attempts and persists at it. Every upload failure state, progress signal, and error recovery message was designed to protect the user's belief that they can complete this application.

Internal User Research · Loan Officer Workflow · Gap 2 Fixed

The Secondary User Nobody Designed For

The loan officer is the most consequential secondary user in this system — and the most completely ignored by the original design. Every submitted application lands on a loan officer's desk. The quality of that handoff determines processing speed, approval rate, and whether the redesign actually improves business outcomes.

Gap 2 Fixed — Loan Officer Operational Workflow Fully Documented

This section addresses the identified gap: the LO internal user profile is fully researched, their operational workflow mapped step-by-step, their mental model of "application readiness" documented, and all LO-facing features explicitly traced to LO research insights.

The Loan Officer As-Is Workflow — Receiving a Submitted Application

01
Generic email: "New mortgage application submitted"

"I get an email that says 'New mortgage application submitted.' Nothing else. No summary, no document count, no loan type, no credit score preview. I have to open the CRM, search for the applicant's name, find the file, and start piecing together what I have." — LO Interview #2[cite: 1]

02
Manual CRM triage — 20–30 minutes per application

LO opens the application and manually works through a personal mental checklist that lives in their head: Is income documented? Are assets verified? Is the property type clear? Is credit pulled? Any flags? This checklist is nowhere in the system. Time per triage: 20–30 min for complete applications, longer for incomplete ones.[cite: 1]

03
Missing document identification — no system visibility

"At least half my applications are missing at least one document. The system does not tell me what is missing — I have to figure it out by comparing what I received against what I know is required for that loan type. Then I call the applicant, who often does not know what I am talking about." — LO Interview #4[cite: 1]

04
Cold outreach call — 34% first-call answer rate

The LO calls the applicant with no advance information about the best time to reach them. Answer rate on first call: approximately 34% (internal call centre data). Each unsuccessful attempt requires a callback, a voicemail, and another attempt.[cite: 1]

05
Unstructured 23-minute call — both parties unprepared

"By the time I get the applicant on the phone, neither of us is prepared. I am reading their file while talking to them. They do not know what I have received. We both end up confused." — LO Interview #1. Mean call duration: 23 minutes. Post-redesign: 11 minutes.[cite: 1]
