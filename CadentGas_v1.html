01 · Research Phase
Understanding the Physiology of Crisis

Before touching a single wireframe, we needed to understand the human body under extreme stress. When adrenaline floods the system, cognition narrows. Working memory shrinks. Typing slows. An interface designed for a calm office worker will catastrophically fail an agent managing a panicked caller reporting a gas leak.

"The agent is not merely a data-entry operator. They are a cognitive bridge between a distressed human and a life-saving system — while simultaneously managing their own emotional regulation." — Research synthesis, Phase 1 observation notes
Method 1 — Contextual Inquiry: Shadowing First Responders

We embedded with call-centre agents during peak hours (6–10 AM, the "gas-ignition window") and observed non-verbal behaviour as primary data. The body does not lie. We catalogued every mouse-hover hesitation, every audible sigh, every moment of backward glancing at post-it notes.

F1
The Hover Phenomenon

Agents hovered over form fields for an average of 2.3 seconds before typing — not from confusion, but from re-reading the label while listening to a caller. This "read-listen-type" tripling is a clear cognitive overload signal the existing UI ignored entirely.

F2
The Sigh Trigger

Agents invariably sighed when the system required them to navigate away to locate a postcode zone or re-enter a previous address. Each redirect broke conversational flow, forcing the caller to repeat information — escalating distress on both sides of the line.

F3
Post-it Note Architecture

Every agent desk featured a personal taxonomy of post-it notes: emergency codes, area abbreviations, engineer names. This is a classic "external cognition offload" — a sign the system's information architecture had failed to internalise what users needed most.

Method 2 — Linguistic Audit of 50+ Emergency Calls

We performed a structured discourse analysis on call recordings, tagging every utterance by emotional valence, information density, and pivot point — the precise moment in a call where the caller's distress peaks and the agent's ability to extract structured data drops to its lowest point.

Critical Finding — The Pivot Point

In 73% of calls, the Critical Pivot Point occurred within 90 seconds of call initiation — typically when the caller was asked to confirm their exact address. Callers under acute stress frequently do not know their precise postcode. The existing system had no fallback. Agents were forced to improvise, causing 8–14 seconds of silence on live emergency calls.

Method 3 — Task Analysis: Counting Every Click

We task-analysed the legacy workflow end-to-end, mapping each click, each form field, and each modal dialogue required to create an emergency ticket.

Critical Field Finding — The 8-Minute Call Problem

Our call recording analysis revealed that emergency calls averaged 4–8 minutes in duration — not because the information was complex, but because callers under acute stress repeatedly loop back to the same details. A caller reporting a gas leak will instinctively repeat their address three times, contradict their own account of the smell, and ask "are you still there?" mid-sentence. This double-loop of repetition — caller repeating due to distress, agent asking to repeat due to interface friction — was responsible for an average of 2.3 additional minutes on every call.

F4
The Repetition Spiral

When agents lost context mid-call (due to navigating away from the main form), 68% of callers re-stated their core problem from the beginning — adding 90–120 seconds to call duration.

F5
The "Are You Still There?" Signal

In 41% of calls, the caller asked "are you still there?" at least once — always during a period of agent silence caused by navigating the CRM. This question is a distress escalator that resets emotional rapport from scratch.

The Legacy System — A Documented Failure Catalogue

Beyond raw click counts, the legacy system embodied seven systemic design failures that compounded each other under stress.

01
No Step-by-Step Structure — Flat Form

26 form fields presented simultaneously on a single scrolling page with no sequence or progress anchors.

02
Illegible Typography

Labels rendered at 10–11px, becoming unreadable under stress-induced pupillary dilation.

03
Illogical Field Ordering

Fields ordered by database column sequence, contradicting the natural flow of spoken emergency calls.

04
Pop-Hover & Modal Dependencies

Critical reference data hidden behind volatile hover tooltips that dismissed on minor mouse drift.

05
Page Load Latency

Address validation triggered full 3.2s reloads; engineer lookup opened separate tabs with 6–8s delays.

06
No Caller Context Persistence

Navigating away from the primary ticket screen risked complete loss of unsaved input data.

07
No Prompting or Call Scripts

Offered no call script or question prompts, forcing reliance on unstandardised informal memory.

Psychological Implication — Cognitive Load Theory

Scores above 70 indicate extraneous load — effort spent fighting the interface. Our mandate: eliminate extraneous load and redirect it to germane load (understanding the emergency).
